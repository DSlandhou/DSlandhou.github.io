 > # Some Thoughts on Competitive Mobile Games
>
> Compared to PC and console titles, mobile games offer more intuitive controls and a vastly more fluid, expansive player base.
>
> Yet games like *Honor of Kings* — notorious for their **meager positive feedback** — are a poor fit for those already starved of affirmation in real life, or for anyone navigating upheaval or the depths of a personal low.
>
> They suit, instead, those who have achieved a kind of **life freedom**:
>
> > People no longer troubled by housing, food, or travel *(domestic or to neighboring countries — I say "neighboring" because a round-trip first-class ticket from the U.S. to Shanghai is already a steeply selective bar, so I narrow the scope)*, nor by the marginal costs of modern existence: utilities, internet, subscription fees, taxes. In short, those whose accumulated fixed capital is sufficient to cover a lifetime of modern living costs.
>
> Only they can play from a position of **elevation**, much as one might scroll TikTok to observe the lives of ordinary people from above.
>
> ---
>
> Competitive games remain team games, yet the team's composition and role assignment hinge on the game company's artificially tuned algorithms. The result is a system of spectacular disorder.
>
> > *"An unfiltered Manhattan would be a slum."*
> >
> > I coined that. Crude, but apt.
>
> Engaging with another human being is an intrusion into the chain of cause and effect. Wealth and social class can metabolize that causality. Or, put another way:
>
> > Keeping people in a zoo is far more civilized than leaving them to forage on the open savanna.
>
> I endorse in-game trash talk. Any objective outlet for releasing pressure and complex emotion has my full support. I practice it myself.
>
> **Positive feedback is vital to a *Homo sapiens*.**
